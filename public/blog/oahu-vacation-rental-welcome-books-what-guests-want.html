O'ahu Vacation Rental Welcome Books: What Guests Actually Want to Know

By The O'ahu Collection Updated: May 2026 11 min read

Most vacation rental welcome books fail at the one job they have: answering the questions guests will ask at 9 PM when they can't figure out how to turn on the TV, or at 7 AM when they're trying to find the nearest coffee shop.

A great welcome book — specific, organized, actually useful — does something powerful: it makes guests feel hosted without requiring you to be there. It prevents the "quick question" messages at inconvenient hours. It stops the 4-star review that says "instructions were confusing." And when done well, it's a genuine differentiator that guests mention in their 5-star reviews: "The welcome book was the best I've ever seen."

This guide covers exactly what to include for an O'ahu vacation rental — from the essential operational details to the local knowledge that separates a good property from a great one.

Physical vs. Digital: Which Format Works Best

Both have genuine advantages. The right answer for most O'ahu vacation rentals is both — a lean physical card for critical info and a QR code linking to a comprehensive digital guide.

Physical Welcome Book

Pros: Accessible without WiFi (guests may struggle to connect on arrival), tangible premium feel, stays in the unit for all guests, no app required.
Cons: Can't be updated without reprinting, can be missed by guests who don't notice it, easy to lose in larger properties.

Best for: Core operational information (WiFi, check-out, emergency contacts, key appliance guides). Keep it to 8–12 pages in a nice binder or folio.

Digital Welcome Guide

Tools like Hostfully, Touch Stay, and Guesty for Hosts offer guest portal systems. A well-built digital guide can be sent in the pre-arrival message and accessed on any device.
Pros: Easy to update, accessible before arrival (guests can plan ahead), searchable, can include clickable links and maps.
Cons: Requires internet access to view, guests need to actually open the link.

The Hybrid Approach (Recommended)

Print a clean, one-page "Quick Start Card" laminated and placed on the kitchen counter. It contains: WiFi credentials, door code, check-out time and top 3 tasks, emergency number, and a QR code linking to the full digital guide. This catches every guest — even those who ignore the pre-arrival message — and directs them to comprehensive information online.

Arrival & Access

The first 15 minutes after a guest arrives are the highest-stakes part of their stay. Make this section impossible to miss and impossible to misunderstand.

  • Door code / lockbox instructions: Step-by-step with photos if the lock is non-obvious
  • Parking: Exactly where to park, how many vehicles, what to display (any permit requirements), where NOT to park
  • WiFi network name and password: In large text, at the front of the book and on the Quick Start Card
  • Elevator or gate codes (if applicable)
  • Property map or floor plan: For larger homes or multi-unit properties
  • Where things are: Towels, extra bedding, beach gear, trash bags, cleaning supplies
  • Noise hours specific to the neighborhood (some HOAs are strict; guests need to know before 10 PM, not after)

House Essentials

These are the operational questions guests ask most frequently. Answer them proactively:

  • Air conditioning: How to adjust — temperature range, fan speed, remote or wall unit. Note: specify that AC should not be set below 68°F (prevents overcooling and energy waste). Many guests from hot climates crank AC unnecessarily.
  • Ceiling fans: Location of wall switches, direction recommendation for cooling
  • Hot water heater: Does it take a minute to warm up? Is there a solar water heater that runs cooler on cloudy days?
  • Trash and recycling: Which bin is which, where to put it, what day pickup is, where to store between pickups
  • Recycling specifics: Hawaii's HI-5 deposit system — guests from the mainland won't know this and will appreciate an explanation
  • Laundry: Location, how to operate (front-load vs. top-load instructions), where detergent is stored
  • Beach gear inventory: List exactly what's available (chairs, umbrella, snorkel sets, cooler, boogie boards) and where it's stored
  • BBQ: How to ignite, where to find propane refills, cleaning expectations

Appliance Guides

Non-obvious appliances cause more guest friction than almost anything else. Don't assume guests know how to use your specific models.

  • TV and streaming: How to turn on, input selection, which streaming services are available and how to access, how to connect their own accounts, how to disconnect before check-out
  • Smart home devices: Voice assistant commands available (if applicable), smart lighting instructions
  • Oven/range: Gas vs. electric, how to light (if gas), special modes, self-cleaning (not during stay)
  • Dishwasher: Where detergent is, correct loading, cycle selection
  • Coffee maker: Type (Keurig? Pour-over? Nespresso?), how to use, where pods/filters/coffee are
  • Pool or hot tub: How to adjust temperature, jets, lights, safety rules, hours
  • Outdoor shower: Common on O'ahu beach properties — hot/cold instructions, rinse-before-entering-pool requirement

House Rules (The Right Way)

House rules are required — but how you present them determines whether guests feel welcomed or processed.

The wrong approach: A dense list of "DO NOT" statements that reads like a legal disclaimer. Guests skim it defensively.

The right approach: Frame rules as hospitality. Explain the why behind each important rule — it converts compliance from resentment to understanding.

Instead of: "NO SHOES INSIDE. Violation may result in additional cleaning fees."

Try: "We keep the home shoe-free — Hawaii style. A shoe rack is at each entrance. This keeps the floors and carpets genuinely clean for you and every guest."

Cover the critical rules:

  • No-shoes policy (standard for Hawaii)
  • Quiet hours (specific times)
  • Maximum occupancy
  • No smoking / vaping (all areas, including lanai)
  • Pet policy (if allowed: where they may be; if not allowed: state clearly)
  • Pool rules (no diving, adults supervise children, no glass)
  • Parking limitations
  • No parties / events
  • Reef-safe sunscreen only (frame this as Hawaii etiquette, not a rule — guests respond better)

Check-Out Instructions

Check-out instructions should be simple, positive in tone, and repeated: once in the main welcome book, once on a small card placed on the kitchen counter the night before check-out (or sent as an automated message).

What to Ask and What NOT to Ask

Hosts sometimes overload check-out with cleaning tasks that cross the line between "reasonable tidying" and "doing the cleaner's job for free." Guests who feel they're cleaning a property they paid to stay in write about it in reviews.

Reasonable to ask:

  • Start a load of towels in the washer (or place in designated laundry bin)
  • Put dishes in dishwasher and start it
  • Remove all food from refrigerator
  • Take out trash to exterior bin
  • Lock all doors and windows
  • Return keys / confirm lock code is locked

Not reasonable to ask (unless you discount for it):

  • Strip all beds (guests perceive this as cleaning work they already paid for)
  • Mop floors or vacuum
  • Clean appliances
  • Multiple loads of laundry

Food & Grocery Recommendations

This section is where most welcome books fall flat — and where yours can genuinely stand out. Generic "try Matsumoto's shave ice!" content that appears in every Waikiki tourist brochure is useless. Guests want to know what you actually recommend.

Grocery Stores (With Specifics)

  • Nearest grocery store, hours, distance
  • Nearest Costco if guests are stocking up (Iwilei or Salt Lake)
  • Nearest Asian grocery (Don Quijote, Times, H Mart) if in range
  • Nearest pharmacy (note if it's a 24-hour location)

Restaurant Recommendations (Be Specific and Honest)

Include 3–5 restaurants in each category with an honest one-line description. Specificity builds trust:

  • Best breakfast: [Name] on [Street] — "the loco moco here is genuinely exceptional, arrive before 9 AM on weekends"
  • Best poke: Not every poke shop is equal — your neighborhood likely has one standout
  • Best plate lunch: For the authentic local experience, not the hotel version
  • Best dinner (nice night out): One mid-range, one splurge
  • Best coffee: Specific to the neighborhood — not Starbucks
  • Best shave ice: Yes, include this — but give the neighborhood option, not just the famous tourist stops

Farmers Markets

Note the nearest market, day and time. Many guests will seek this out if you mention it — and it creates a great local experience they'll talk about in their review.

Beaches & Water Access

For most O'ahu vacation rental guests, beaches are the primary reason for the trip. Make this section detailed and practical.

  • Nearest beach: Name, distance, what makes it good (calm water, snorkeling, surfing, etc.)
  • Parking tips: Where to park, what time to arrive to get a spot on weekends, any free vs. paid lots
  • Water conditions: Brief seasonal note — e.g., "North Shore waves are large October through April — this is surf viewing, not swimming beach in winter"
  • Snorkeling spots: If you're near a reef, note it with specific entry point
  • Lifeguard vs. no lifeguard: Important safety information, especially for families
  • Beach gear in the unit: Remind them what's available (chairs, umbrella, snorkel sets) and where it's stored
  • Reef-safe sunscreen reminder: Frame positively — "Hawaii's reefs are protected by law. We keep reef-safe sunscreen at the property — please use it or bring your own."

Activities & Experiences

Keep this section curated — 8–12 recommendations across categories, not an exhaustive tourist directory. Quality of curation matters more than quantity.

  • Hikes: 1–2 per difficulty level, with specific trailhead location and honest difficulty assessment
  • Snorkeling: Best accessible spots beyond the nearest beach
  • Surfing lessons: If applicable to your neighborhood
  • Boat charters: A specific referral (not a generic "there are many charter operators")
  • Cultural sites: Bishop Museum, Iolani Palace, Pearl Harbor — note which require advance reservations
  • Day trips: North Shore drive, Manoa Falls, Kailua for East-side properties — with honest timing (allow a full day)

Neighborhood Notes

A brief, honest neighborhood orientation helps guests feel oriented and avoids the "we didn't know the area" complaint.

  • Name of the neighborhood and general character
  • Walking distance to anything notable (beach, coffee, restaurant, store)
  • Anything guests should know — busy road nearby, community pool hours, HOA quiet hours
  • Parking situation in the neighborhood beyond the property
  • Best time to drive to Waikiki / Town if applicable (avoid rush hour, which runs 7–9 AM and 3:30–6 PM)

Emergency Information

This section should be impossible to miss — put it at the front of the physical book and at the top of the digital guide.

  • Emergency contact (host or property manager): Name and phone number
  • Nearest emergency room: Hospital name, address, estimated drive time
  • Nearest urgent care: For non-emergency medical needs
  • Nearest pharmacy: With 24-hour note if applicable
  • Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222
  • Tsunami warning system: Brief note on what to do if sirens sound (O'ahu has monthly siren tests — note that the first Friday of each month at 11:45 AM is a TEST so guests aren't alarmed)
  • Location of fire extinguisher in unit
  • Location of first aid kit in unit

Design & Presentation Tips

A well-designed welcome book communicates premium without requiring expensive production.

  • Use the property name or brand — "Welcome to Hale Malu" feels more like an arrival than "Vacation Rental Welcome Guide"
  • Include one beautiful photo of the property or the nearest beach on the cover page
  • Keep it scannable — headers, bullet points, short paragraphs. Nobody reads dense prose in a welcome book.
  • Print quality matters — print at FedEx Office on matte cardstock, not on a home inkjet printer. The difference is immediately visible and communicates care.
  • Update it twice a year — restaurant closures, changed WiFi passwords, and outdated opening hours create frustrating guest experiences
  • Add a personal welcome note — two to three sentences in your own voice welcoming guests and expressing genuine hope they enjoy O'ahu. It's a small touch that makes the property feel like a home, not a transaction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a vacation rental welcome book actually improve reviews?

Yes — significantly. Properties with comprehensive welcome books receive fewer mid-stay messages, fewer negative reviews citing confusing instructions, and higher communication scores. A well-designed welcome book turns guest frustration into self-service satisfaction.

What should a vacation rental welcome book include?

WiFi credentials, check-out instructions, appliance guides, house rules summary, emergency contacts, parking details, trash and recycling procedures, nearest grocery stores, local restaurant recommendations, beach and activity recommendations, and neighborhood orientation.

Should I use a physical book or a digital welcome guide?

The best approach for O'ahu vacation rentals is both: a brief printed Quick Start Card with essentials plus a QR code linking to a comprehensive digital guide. The card catches guests who don't check their pre-arrival message; the digital guide provides depth.

What local O'ahu information should I include?

Guests want honest, specific local knowledge — not generic tourist guide content. Include your personal restaurant recommendations, the nearest beach and how to access it, where to buy groceries, the best nearby farmers market, and any neighborhood-specific tips. Specific = useful. Generic = ignored.

How long should a vacation rental welcome book be?

8–15 pages for a physical version, or organized into clear sections for a digital guide. Shorter books miss important information; longer books (30+ pages) don't get read. Organize by section with clear headers.
